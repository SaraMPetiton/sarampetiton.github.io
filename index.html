Sara Petiton

I recently obtained my PhD in Machine Learning applied to neuroimaging and biomarker discovery at Université Paris-Saclay. My research was conducted at NeuroSpin (CEA Paris-Saclay) in the GAIA lab, under the supervision of Edouard Duchesnay and advisorship of Antoine Grigis.

My thesis, entitled "Machine and deep learning methods for identifying neuroanatomical biomarkers of bipolar disorder," (full manuscript here) explored which features and models can uncover disease-related specificities from brain imaging. It addressed how to deal with small medical datasets, how to assess which part of the signal is reproducible across scanner sites, and how to make models interpretable to clinicians while keeping them grounded in real anatomy rather than confounding variables in the data.

Prior to my PhD, I worked as a software engineer at Intel following my master's internship there, building machine learning models for CPU performance prediction.

Publications

  • Exploring the potential of representation and transfer learning for anatomical neuroimaging: Application to psychiatry

    Dufumier B, Gori P, Petiton S, Louiset R, Mangin JF, Grigis A, Duchesnay E. · NeuroImage, 2024

    Work demonstrating improved deep learning classification of psychiatric illness vs. healthy neuroanatomy when dealing with small psychiatric datasets and whole-brain, voxel-wise anatomical MRI as input features. This is achieved using transfer learning with a contrastive model pretrained on a large cohort of healthy controls using age as a proxy signal.

    Paper

  • How and why does deep ensemble coupled with transfer learning increase performance in bipolar disorder and schizophrenia classification?

    Petiton S, Grigis A, Dufumier B, Duchesnay E. · IEEE International Symposium on Biomedical Imaging (ISBI), 2024

    Conference paper demonstrating that deep learning models for psychiatric classification using transfer learning, initialized with the same pretraining weights from a model trained on healthy controls, outperform randomly initialized models and offer greater reproducibility because their loss stays within the same basin of the loss landscape. Additionally, we benchmarked deep ensembles to determine the exact number of models needed to achieve the optimal tradeoff between model count, performance gains, and uncertainty minimization.

    Paper

  • Surface and Voxel-based Brain Morphometric Representations are Synergistic for Individual-Level Prediction of Bipolar Disorder

    Petiton S, Grigis A, Dufumier B, Wessa M, Brambilla P, Polosan M, McDonald C, Piguet C, Phillips ML, Eyler L, Chavas J, Frouin V, Houenou J, Favre P, Laidi C, Bourgin J, Duchesnay E. · Preprint, under review

    While model choice is widely examined in neuroimaging classification, feature representations are rarely analyzed despite different morphometries capturing distinct neuroanatomical properties. In this paper, we compared performance and uncertainties across machine learning and deep learning models using voxel-based (VBM) and surface-based morphometric (SBM) features at the voxel, vertex, and region of interest levels. Classifying bipolar disorder vs. healthy controls across a 763-subject multisite dataset using leave-one-site-out cross-validation, we found that VBM features consistently outperform SBM features regardless of model type. Moreover, combining both morphometries across granularities yields synergistic gains: our combined model reached 76.76% ROC-AUC, an average 5.20% ROC-AUC improvement over the best single-feature classifiers, while also increasing robustness.

    Preprint

  • Improving clinical interpretability of linear neuroimaging models through feature whitening

    Petiton S, Grigis A, Vock R, Duchesnay E. · arXiv preprint

    This work proposes a whitening approach for linear neuroimaging classifiers that addresses a specific interpretability problem. While linear models are often regarded as the most interpretable machine learning models, strong correlations or collinearity in the input features can skew model weights, and obscure which regions are truly driving the predictions. In neuroimaging data, features are often highly correlated, especially homologous left and right hemisphere structures, causing linear model weights to reflect shared rather than region-specific contributions. By decorrelating anatomically informed pairs of regions while retaining the full input signal, unlike PCA or ICA which use whitening for dimensionality reduction, the method highlights which individual regions drive a prediction without changing model accuracy. This approach was evaluated on bipolar disorder and schizophrenia classification from ROI-based features, with model weights validated against ENIGMA meta-analytic findings.

    Preprint

  • Hippocampal and Amygdalar Atrophy Predict Lithium Response in Bipolar Disorder: Machine Learning Evidence from the R-LiNK Prospective Multi-Site Cohort

    Petiton S, Bourgin J, Brambilla P, Andreassen O, Bauer M, Bayas M, Boumezbeur F, Bourdon C, Cattaneo A, Chupin M, Colom F, Cousins D, Dubertret C, Dufumier B, Dupont T, Etain B, Favre P, Fico G, Grigis A, Haffen E, Hennion V, Houenou J, Kobayashi N, Laidi C, Lambert J, Landen M, Marie-Claire C, Mombelli E, Nuß I, Oedegaard KJ, Oltedal L, Reif A, Resche-Rigon M, Rossi R, Schulze T, Squarcina L, Thelwall P, Tønning M, Kessing LV, Vieta E, Vinberg M, Young A, Bellivier F, Duchesnay E.· Preprint, under review

    Part of the R-LiNK European project, this work showed hippocampus and amygdalar atrophy to be predictive of positive treatment response to lithium in bipolar disorder. Using structural MRI from 116 patients prior to treatment, a linear regularized classifier predicted response with a ROC-AUC of 0.69, with the hippocampus and amygdala alone reaching 0.67 and 0.64 respectively. Extensive feature importance analyses of the model showed these two regions to be specific predictors of response. Normative modeling against a healthy reference sample of over 50,000 participants showed that good responders deviated more from typical neuroanatomy at baseline in these regions than partial or non-responders. Lithium increased gray matter volume brain-wide across all patients regardless of response, and because good responders started out more atrophied, this increase brought their hippocampal and amygdalar volumes closer to normative levels. Effects were consistently stronger in males than in females, suggesting a sex-dependent component to lithium's mechanism of action.

    Preprint
